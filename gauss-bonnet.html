The Gauss–Bonnet theorem

Bend a surface however you like: its total curvature only cares about how many holes it has.

∫M K dA = 2π χ(M)

The left side: geometry

M is a closed surface: a sphere, a doughnut, a pretzel, anything without edges. At every point it has a Gaussian curvature K. K is positive where the surface bulges like a dome, negative where it curves like a saddle, and zero where it is flat, or bent in only one direction like a cylinder.

The integral adds up the curvature over the whole surface: the total curvature. To compute it you need to measure lengths and angles, so it is pure geometry.

The right side: topology

χ(M) is the Euler characteristic. Draw a mesh of triangles on the surface and count: χ = V − E + F (vertices − edges + faces). For a surface with g holes, χ = 2 − 2g. You don't need any measurements, only counting. It's an integer, and it doesn't change if you stretch or squash the surface.

Why it's remarkable

The theorem says these two things are always equal. Something you can only compute with a ruler and a protractor turns out to depend only on the number of holes.

  • Sphere of radius r: K = 1/r² everywhere and the area is 4πr², so the total is 4π. Since χ = 2, the right side is 2π·2 = 4π. ✓
  • Squash the sphere into a potato: the curvature moves around, more at the bumps and less at the flat parts, but the total stays exactly 4π.
  • Doughnut (torus): χ = 0, so the positive curvature on the outside exactly cancels the negative curvature on the inside.

A version you can walk

On a flat sheet, a triangle's angles add up to π (180°). On a curved surface they don't, and the difference is exactly the curvature inside the triangle. Walk from the North Pole down to the equator, turn 90°, walk a quarter of the way around the Earth, turn 90° and walk back up. That triangle has three right angles (270°), and the extra 90° is the curvature of the eighth of the globe you enclosed.
