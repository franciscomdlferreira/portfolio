The Brouwer fixed-point theorem

However you continuously move a disc onto itself, some point stays put.

The statement

Let D be a closed disc (a filled circle, or a solid ball in any dimension) and let f : D → D be any continuous function, meaning one that doesn't tear anything. Then there is at least one point x with

f(x) = x

That point is called a fixed point.

Everyday versions

  • Crumple a map of Lisbon and drop it on a table in Lisbon. At least one point on the map is exactly above the real place it represents.
  • Gently stir a cup of coffee. In an idealised version, where the liquid moves continuously and stays in the cup, at every moment some point of the coffee is exactly where it started.

The proof in the drawing

Suppose f has no fixed point, so f(x) ≠ x for every x. Then for each x you can draw the ray that starts at f(x) and passes through x, and follow it until it hits the edge of the disc. Call that point g(x).

This g moves smoothly as x moves, and points already on the edge stay where they are. So g continuously squashes the whole disc onto its boundary circle without moving the circle.

That's impossible. Take the loop that goes once around the edge. Inside the disc it can be shrunk to a point, and applying g to that shrinking would shrink it inside the circle. But on a circle, a loop that winds once around can never be shrunk, because π₁(circle) = ℤ (the same fundamental group that appears in Van Kampen). Contradiction, so a fixed point must exist.

Why economists care

The theorem says a solution exists without telling you where it is. John Nash used exactly this kind of argument to prove that every finite game has an equilibrium, and Arrow and Debreu used fixed-point theorems to show that an economy can have prices where supply meets demand in every market.
